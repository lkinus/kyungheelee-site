1. Information Architecture
Too much effort to get to content
Users had to take too many steps before they could start watching.
Guide became the default path
When the broader experience felt confusing, users returned to the familiar live TV guide.
Content was harder to find and manage
Users missed On Demand content and struggled to manage DVR or saved content.
The evidence pointed to three IA breakdowns.
Both models had trade-offs. The category-based model offered familiar entry points, but continued to mix live and on-demand content and duplicate paths. The goal-and-state model organized destinations around what users wanted to do and whether content was live, on demand, or saved.I chose the model that best addressed Sling’s core IA problem: reducing overlap and making each destination more predictable.
The key IA decision was how to organize content around user intent.
Option A · Category-based
▪ Movies, Shows, and Sports as primary destinations ▪ Familiar content categories ▪ Continued overlap between live and on-demand content
Option B · Goal-and-state-based
▪ Guide for live TV ▪ On Demand for VOD browsing ▪ Library for saved and recorded content ▪ Clearer, more distinct destination roles
Senior leadership wanted DVR to remain highly visible because DVR upgrades generated revenue. I advocated for grouping recorded and saved content in Library, where users expected to find it.
The key alignment challenge was balancing DVR visibility with IA clarity.
The trade-off
DVR supported upgrade revenue, but a separate top-level destination risked adding complexity to an already crowded navigation model.
My position
Group DVR, My List, My Channels, and saved content under Library so users had one clear saved-content destination.
How we aligned
Use Library as the primary saved-content destination, preserve DVR exposure through Home modules, and test whether DVR needed a top-level entry after launch.
The new structure reduced overlapping destinations and created clearer paths to start with relevant content, watch live TV, explore on-demand content, return to saved content, or search directly.
The new IA organized Sling around clearer user goals.
The result was a simpler five-destination model. ▪ Home — Personalized, promotional, and curated content across live and on-demand. ▪ Guide — Familiar live TV browsing for cable-oriented users. ▪ On Demand — A dedicated destination for Sling’s on-demand library. ▪ Library — Saved content, favorites, watchlists, and DVR. ▪ Search — Direct access for users who already knew what they wanted.
After simplifying the IA, I compared top and left navigation against the needs of a content-heavy TV experience: preserving screen space, supporting frequent destination switching, and remaining predictable with a remote.
The navigation model had to balance immersion and wayfinding.
2. Main Navigation
A collapsible left rail better matched Sling’s frequent task switching. It preserved content space when collapsed, made core destinations easier to reach, and created a clearer boundary between navigation and browsing.
I chose left navigation for Sling’s TV experience.
Preserved browsing space
A collapsed left rail used less vertical space than top navigation on TV.
Faster destination switching
Users could quickly move between Guide, DVR, Search, On Demand, and Home with a remote.
Clear navigation mode
Expanding the rail made it clear that users were switching destinations, not browsing content.
Market signal
Six of eight paid streaming services reviewed used left navigation, and 56% of participants preferred it.
Top navigation offered greater visual immersion, but made destination switching heavier once users were deep in content. Because Sling’s larger problem was wayfinding, I prioritized faster, more predictable access to destinations over maximum visual immersion.
Trade-off
With the IA and navigation defined, I compared two structural models for how browsing and playback should coexist. The decision had to balance usability, cross-platform feasibility, ad viewability, and future viewing continuity.
The redesign needed a base model for browsing and playback.
3. UI & Video Foundation
Video overlay UI (Lean-forward)
Video behind UI (Lean-back)
After comparing both models, Video Behind UI offered a more continuous live TV experience, while Video Overlay UI provided clearer separation between browsing and playback, lower delivery risk, and stronger protection for ad viewability.
I chose the model that balanced usability, feasibility, and ad viewability.
We traded the more continuous, always-on live TV feel of Video Behind UI, which kept playback visible during browsing, for the clearer separation of Video Overlay UI. The selected model fit Sling’s existing architecture, protected ad viewability, and created a more consistent foundation across platforms while still supporting future continuity patterns such as picture-in-picture and muted preview.
Trade-off
With the IA, navigation, and video foundation defined, I reviewed platform guidance and common streaming TV conventions, then established consistent behavior for Sling’s remote-based experience.
The rules defined behavior across four interaction areas. • Focus movement: Predictable D-pad movement across content and controls. • Navigation entry and exit: Consistent movement between the side menu and browsing surfaces. • Back behavior: Clear expectations for returning to the previous state. • State transitions: Consistent handoffs between browsing, detail, playback, and controls.
I turned familiar TV interaction patterns into shared rules.

Shared screen models, reusable patterns, and platform guidance.
With the foundation in place, the next challenge was helping users decide what to watch faster. Discovery was not about showing more content—it was about matching the right surface and content to the user’s intent.
Three surfaces served three different intents. • Home — What should I watch now? • On Demand — What can I explore deeper? • Search — I know what I want—help me get there fast.Home became the primary discovery surface, while On Demand supported deeper exploration and Search supported high-intent tasks.
Make discovery relevant by default.
Decision 2 · Personalized Discovery
After improving navigation and discovery, the next challenge was preserving viewing context. Users needed to browse, evaluate, and switch live content without stopping playback, losing context, or committing too early.
Protect viewing momentum across browsing and playback.
I defined three complementary patterns around the moments where viewing momentum broke most: browsing while watching, evaluating content, and switching live channels.
I connected browsing and playback into a more continuous viewing flow.
Browsing became part of the viewing flow—not an interruption to it.
